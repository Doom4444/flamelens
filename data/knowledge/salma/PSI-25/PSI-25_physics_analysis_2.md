## PSI-25 | 1. Summary
- BASS-II studies how solid fuels ignite, grow a flame, spread a flame, and extinguish in low-velocity forced flows in microgravity, on the ISS in the Microgravity Science Glovebox. [PSI]
- Primary variables: forced flow velocity (speed and direction), ambient oxygen concentration (set by nitrogen vitiation of the working volume), and sample geometry (rods, spherical section, slabs, films, fabric sheets). [PSI]
- Core hypothesis: with adequate ventilation, a material burns as well or better in microgravity than in normal gravity at otherwise identical conditions. The 1g upward-burning NASA test may not be conservative for flammability in space. [PSI]
- [N/A] The PSI-25 record reports no measured results (no spread rates, extinction limits, or flame sizes).
- The supplied test log has 129 rows and 6 investigators. It records the sample, the flow settings, and the oxygen, CO2, and CO readings before and after each test, with notes on flow changes, extinction, and blowoff. [TABLE]

## PSI-25 | 2. Physical question
Where is the fundamental limit of flammability for a given solid material in microgravity, as a function of flow speed and oxygen concentration? The record says testing at constant O2 mole fractions allows studying flame spread and extinction at low and high flow speed, and that the aim is to find the fundamental limit for a given material. [PSI]

Objectives stated in the record: [PSI]
1. Study ignition, flame growth, flame spread, and extinction limits for solid fuels in low-velocity forced flows in microgravity.
2. Begin to bridge the gap between the normal-gravity NASA-STD-6001 Test #1 method, ground-based microgravity tests, and actual material flammability in microgravity.
3. Provide SoFIE PIs with preliminary data to refine Science Requirements.
4. Examine practical, realistic (thicker) fuels in typical geometries, including slabs, cylinders, and spherical sections.

## PSI-25 | 4. Physical explanation
This section explains the physics behind what the record says. Statements tagged [PHYS] are standard combustion physics, not PSI-25 results.

### 4.1 Buoyancy and flame shape
- [PSI] In outer space, fuels burn as oval balls rather than with an upward-pointed cone flame as on Earth. This simpler combustion process lets researchers evaluate computer models of fuel burning.
- [PHYS] On Earth, hot gases rise and drive a buoyant flow around the flame, which gives the pointed upward shape. In microgravity this flow is absent, so the flame is shaped only by the imposed flow and by the flow the flame itself creates. The flow field is simpler, which is why it is a good test for models.

### 4.2 Why Test 1 may not be conservative
- [PSI] NASA tests and controls materials with an upward-burning test in normal gravity, considered the worst-case geometry for flammability on Earth. It may not be conservative for flammability in space.
- [PHYS] In 1g upward burning, the flame spreads in the direction of the buoyant flow, a geometry that favors fast spread on Earth. In microgravity the flow is whatever is imposed, so a material can behave differently. A material that passes the 1g screening can still burn in a low-flow microgravity condition. This is the reason for testing at controlled flow speeds and oxygen levels.

### 4.3 The role of flow speed: why there are two kinds of limit
- [PSI] Testing at constant O2 mole fractions allows studying flame spread and extinction at low and high flow speed.
- [PHYS] At low flow, the oxygen supply to the flame is small. Heat loss from the flame (including radiation) becomes large compared with the heat the flame produces, and the flame can go out (a quenching limit). At high flow, the flame is pushed away and cooled by the fast stream, and can be blown off (a blowoff limit). Between the two there is a range of flow where the flame burns. For a given material and oxygen level, the low-flow and high-flow limits together define the flammability range. This is the physical meaning of "the fundamental limit for a given material".
- [TABLE] The log notes both events: "blowoff" appears in 7 rows (B18, B19, O7, O8b, O9, O11, O22), and many rows end a decreasing flow sequence with "ext" or "extinguished" (for example B14, B21, O4, O8a, P5, P9, P10, P13a, P13b, P22a, P22b, P25). One row (O12, 3/8" clear rod, initial O2 13.6%) notes "no ign at any flow", and one (P8, 3/8" black rod, initial O2 16.4%) notes "would not stay lit".
- [TABLE] Reading of the log: in many rod rows, the Air display lists decreasing numbers (for example "5, 0.36, 0.32, 0.31, 0.305") and ends with "ext". This pattern looks like a stepwise reduction of flow until extinction. The log does not define the columns or the units, so no extinction speed is quoted here. [N/A]

### 4.4 The role of oxygen: nitrogen vitiation
- [PSI] Oxygen is varied below 21% by adding nitrogen to the working volume.
- [PHYS] Nitrogen is inert. Replacing part of the air with nitrogen lowers the oxygen fraction, which lowers the reaction rate and the flame temperature. Below some oxygen level the flame cannot be sustained. This is why the extinction limit depends on oxygen as well as on flow.
- [TABLE] Initial O2 in the log runs from 13.6% to 22.2% by volume (126 rows with a value). 82 rows start below 20%, 3 rows at 20.0 to 20.1%, and 41 rows at 20.5% or above. The lowest values are in rod tests (Olson and Fernandez-Pello).

### 4.5 Geometry and thickness
- [PSI] Geometries: rods, spherical section, slabs, films, fabric sheets. Thicker fuels in typical geometries are a stated objective.
- [PHYS] A thin film is heated through its whole thickness almost at once, so its burning is controlled by the gas-phase flame. A thick solid has a temperature gradient through its thickness and takes longer to heat up, so its behavior depends on conduction into the solid. Rods and spherical sections add curvature, which changes how the flow wraps around the sample and how the flame sits on it.
- [TABLE] Sample sizes in the log: PMMA films 0.1 to 0.4 mm thick, 1 cm or 2 cm wide (Bhattacharjee); PMMA 1 to 5 mm thick, 1 cm or 2 cm wide (Miller); rods of 1/4 inch, 3/8 inch, and 0.5 (1/2) inch (Olson and Fernandez-Pello; labeled clear or black); SIBAL 1 cm and 2 cm (T'ien); 4 cm spherical section (T'ien, 2 rows); Nomex III natural (Ferkul, 3 rows); Japan Wax candle (Ferkul, 1 row).
- [N/A] The record does not define "SIBAL", the "1 sided" and "2 sided" labels, or the suffixes "m" and "M" on Sample #.

### 4.6 Opposed and concurrent flow
- [PSI] Flame appearance, growth, and spread rates were determined in both the opposed and the concurrent flow.
- [PHYS] In opposed flow the air comes toward the flame, so oxygen reaches the flame front first. In concurrent flow the air moves with the spread, and the flame lies over the unburned fuel ahead of it. See PSI-26 section 4.4 for the comparison.
- [N/A] The log does not state the flow direction of each test.

### 4.7 Gas analysis: O2, CO2, CO
- [TABLE] For each test the log gives calibrated initial and final O2 (% by volume), initial and final CO2 (% by volume), and initial and final CO (ppm).
- [PHYS] Burning consumes oxygen and releases CO2. CO appears when oxidation is incomplete, for example when oxygen is limited or when the flame is quenched. These gases build up in the working volume, so the change depends on how much fuel burned and for how long, as well as on the conditions. They are not a direct measure of flame intensity.
- [TABLE] The working volume is not reset between some consecutive tests: the initial value of a test equals the final value of the test before it (for example CO 496 ppm in O5a to O5b, 536 ppm in O8a to O8b, 950 ppm in O13a to O13b).
- [TABLE] Radiometer gain (1000, 250, 50, or 10) is recorded for each test. [N/A] The radiometer readings are not in the log.

### 4.8 Radiation
- [PSI, from PSI-26] A critical element of the understanding of flammability is radiative heat emission from the flame.
- [PHYS] Radiative loss from the flame competes with the heat fed back to the fuel, and matters most in slow flows. This is part of the reason for the low-flow quenching limit in 4.3.

## PSI-25 | 9. Questions a team may ask
Q: What is BASS-II?
A: Burning and Suppression of Solids II, an ISS investigation in the Microgravity Science Glovebox (01-01-2010 to 12-31-2022) on ignition, flame growth, flame spread, and extinction limits of solid fuels in low-velocity forced flows. [PSI]

Q: What are the primary variables?
A: Forced flow velocity (speed and direction), ambient oxygen concentration (via nitrogen vitiation), and sample geometry (rods, spherical section, slabs, films, fabric sheets). [PSI]

Q: What is the hypothesis?
A: With adequate ventilation, materials in microgravity burn as well or better than in normal gravity at identical pressure, O2 concentration, and temperature. The 1g upward-burning test may not be conservative for space. [PSI]

Q: Why is the 1g test possibly not conservative?
A: It is the worst-case geometry on Earth, where buoyant flow drives the spread. In microgravity the flow is imposed, so a material can behave differently. [PSI + PHYS]

Q: Why vary the flow speed and the oxygen?
A: To find the fundamental limit of a material. Low flow can quench the flame (little oxygen, large heat loss), high flow can blow it off, and low oxygen lowers flame temperature and reaction rate. [PSI + PHYS]

Q: What is the maximum flow speed?
A: Up to 40 cm/s. [PSI]

Q: How low did the oxygen go in the log?
A: The initial O2 runs from 13.6% to 22.2% by volume. 82 of 126 rows start below 20%. [TABLE]

Q: Did the flames blow off or extinguish?
A: The log notes "blowoff" in 7 rows (B18, B19, O7, O8b, O9, O11, O22) and "ext" or "extinguished" in many others. O12 notes "no ign at any flow". Numerical limits are [N/A] because units are not defined.

Q: Which materials were tested?
A: In the log: PMMA films, PMMA 1 to 5 mm thick, rods of 1/4, 3/8, and 1/2 inch, SIBAL, a 4 cm spherical section, Nomex III natural, and a Japan Wax candle. The record lists rods, spherical sections, slabs, films, and fabric sheets. [PSI + TABLE]

Q: Which test type produced the largest change in CO and O2?
A: PMMA 1 to 5 mm thick (Miller): median O2 drop 1.1% and median CO rise 339.5 ppm. Thin films and SIBAL are lowest. These are changes of gas in the working volume, so they depend on burned mass. [TABLE + PHYS]

Q: Who ran the tests?
A: Bhattacharjee (22 rows), Ferkul (4), Miller (20), Olson (27), Fernandez-Pello (27), and T'ien (29). Olson is not listed among the PSI-25 contacts. [TABLE + PSI]

Q: How many tests and when?
A: 129 rows, 30 test days, mostly 02/14/2014 to 08/04/2014, plus 3 rows in 2016 and 2 rows dated 2018. [TABLE]

Q: How does BASS-II differ from BASS?
A: See table 8.1. The hypothesis is the same. BASS-II lists more geometries, varies oxygen with nitrogen, and has a log with flow and gas data. [PSI + TABLE]

Q: How does PSI-62 relate?
A: It models concurrent flame spread (UFGM) using BASS and BASS-II as benchmarks, with 3D soot and temperature reconstructed from imagery. [PSI]

Q: What were the results?
A: [N/A] The record gives none. The log has conditions and gas readings, not spread rates.

Q: Is the log reliable?
A: It has implausible final O2 values (B2, P22a, P22b, T28a, T28b), two 2018 dates, one GMT mismatch (O10), text in numeric cells, and missing tests (section 7). Check the source before quoting exact values. [TABLE]

## PSI-25 | 13. Terminology and synonyms
General terms, not results. They are listed so that a search with another wording still finds this file.
- PSI-25 = BASS-II = BASS II = Burning and Suppression of Solids II. PSI-26 = BASS.
- Microgravity = low gravity = reduced gravity = 0g = weightlessness (on the ISS).
- Flame spread = flame propagation over a solid. Spread rate = flame spread velocity.
- Concurrent flow = co-current flow (air with the spread). Opposed flow = counter-current flow (air against the spread).
- Extinction = flame-out = quenching (loss of the flame). Blowoff = blow-off (flame removed by a fast flow). In the log, "ext" is written for extinction.
- Oxygen concentration = O2 mole fraction = O2 % by volume. Nitrogen vitiation = nitrogen dilution = oxygen reduction with nitrogen.
- CO = carbon monoxide (ppm). CO2 = carbon dioxide (% by volume).
- Rod = cylindrical sample (barreau). Slab = thick flat sample. Film = thin flat sample. Spherical section = part of a sphere. Fabric sheet = thin cloth sample.
