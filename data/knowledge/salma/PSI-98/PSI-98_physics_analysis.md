# PSI-98: Physics analysis

## PSI-98 | 0. Identification
PSI-98 is Saffire-I (written "Saffire-1" and "Saffire-I" in the record): a large-scale fire experiment on the Orbital Science's Cygnus resupply vehicle after it deberthed from the ISS. Principal investigator David L. Urban (NASA GRC). ## PSI-98 | 1. Summary
- Saffire-I burned a large piece of fabric in a flow duct inside an unmanned spacecraft, to see how fast a large fire grows in low gravity and where the low-gravity flammability limits lie compared with NASA's normal-gravity material flammability screening test. [PSI]
- Sample: SIBAL, a composite fabric of cotton on a fiberglass substrate (75% cotton, 25% fiberglass by weight), about 0.94 m long and 0.4 m wide. Air flow velocity: 20 cm/s. [PSI]
- Hypothesis: terrestrial fires and fire standards carry an inherent risk when used to design spacecraft fire safety systems, because fire behavior in normal and reduced gravity differs greatly and microgravity data are lacking. [PSI]
- The experimental table lists 2 tests: S1 (concurrent flow, burn time 420 s) and S2 (opposed flow, burn time 70 s). Both used 20 cm/s, 182 W for 8 s ignition, and about 21.5% O2. [TABLE]
- [N/A] The record gives no results: no flame growth rate, no flame size, no flammability limit.

## PSI-98 | 2. Physical question
How rapidly does a large-scale fire grow in low gravity, and how do the low-gravity flammability limits compare with those obtained in NASA's normal-gravity material flammability screening test? [PSI] A fire this large cannot be run safely with a crew aboard, so the experiment is done in an expendable spacecraft. [PSI]

## PSI-98 | 3. Experimental set-up
- Platform: the Saffire-1 experiments were performed on the Orbital Science's Cygnus resupply vehicle after it deberthed from the ISS. [PSI]
- Sample: SIBAL (cotton on a fiberglass substrate, 75/25 by weight), about 0.94 m long and 0.4 m wide, simulating a large piece of flammable fabric. SIBAL burns like cotton but keeps its structural integrity after the cotton is consumed, and has been used in many space experiments. [PSI]
- Flow duct: the flight unit has a sample card in the middle of a large flow duct. Air is drawn through the duct by fans at the top, with flow straighteners at the bottom of the experiment module. [PSI]
- Flow: 20 cm/s. [PSI]
- Measurements: two cameras (flame images); thermocouples (flow, flame, and plume temperatures); two radiometers (flame radiation); solid state sensors (pressure, CO2 and O2 mole fraction); two anemometers (flow velocity). All data were compressed and downlinked. [PSI]
- [N/A] The record does not give the ignition method, the ignition power, or the sample thickness. The table gives the ignition power and time (section 6).

## PSI-98 | 4. Physical explanation
This section explains the physics behind what the record says. Statements tagged [PHYS] are standard combustion physics, not PSI-98 results.

### 4.1 Why a large-scale fire in a spacecraft
- [PSI] There is a significant lack of microgravity data for spacecraft designers. The approach so far has been successful, but the uncertainty and risk grow as exploration missions go farther from Earth with longer transit times.
- [PHYS] Flame size, radiation to the fuel, and the heat that preheats unburned fuel all depend on the scale of the fire. A small sample in a glovebox cannot show how a fire grows over a large surface. A large sample (about 0.38 m2) allows the flame to grow over a length that approaches a real piece of fabric. The test cannot be done with a crew present, which is why the record uses an unmanned vehicle.

### 4.2 The role of ventilation flow
- [PSI] Air is drawn through the duct by fans, at 20 cm/s.
- [PHYS] In microgravity there is no buoyant flow, so the flow over the sample is the one the fans impose (plus the flow created by the flame). In a spacecraft, ventilation is the main airflow, so a duct with a fixed flow speed represents a realistic condition. The BASS records (PSI-26, PSI-25) state the same idea as a hypothesis: with adequate ventilation, a material burns as well or better in microgravity.

### 4.3 Why SIBAL
- [PSI] SIBAL burns like cotton but keeps its structural integrity after the cotton is consumed.
- [PHYS] The fiberglass substrate stays in place, so the sample keeps its shape and position while the cotton burns. The flame is then easier to follow and to model. The same fabric is used in the BASS test logs (PSI-26 and PSI-25 list SIBAL samples), which allows comparison between small-scale and large-scale tests.

### 4.4 Concurrent and opposed flow
- [TABLE] S1 is a concurrent-flow test (burn time 420 s). S2 is an opposed-flow test (burn time 70 s). The PSI-98 text does not mention opposed flow, while the PSI-99 and PSI-100 records say the flow was either concurrent or opposed.
- [PHYS] In concurrent flow the air moves with the flame spread, so the flame lies over the unburned fabric ahead of it and heats it. In opposed flow the air comes toward the flame, and oxygen reaches the flame front first. The two directions are different flame-spread regimes. See PSI-26 section 4.4 for the comparison.
- [N/A] The table does not say that the sample burned completely, or give the flame front position over time. The burn times (420 s and 70 s) therefore cannot be converted into spread rates.

### 4.5 Ignition and oxygen
- [TABLE] Ignition power 182 W for 8 s in both tests. The product is 1456 J (computed by the analyst, nominal energy input; the table does not say what the 182 W is measured on).
- [TABLE] Percent O2: "21.5 - 21.7" (S1) and "~ 21.5" (S2).
- [PSI] The results are meant to show how microgravity and limited oxygen affect flame size. [N/A] The two tests are at nearly the same oxygen level, so no oxygen dependence can be read from the table.

### 4.6 What each instrument tells
- [PSI] Cameras give flame images; thermocouples give flow, flame, and plume temperatures; radiometers record flame radiation; solid state sensors measure pressure, CO2 and O2 mole fraction; anemometers track flow velocity.
- [PHYS] Cameras give flame size and position. Thermocouples give the temperature field around the flame. Radiometers give the radiative output of the fire. CO2 and O2 show how much oxygen is consumed and how much product is released in the closed volume. Anemometers check that the actual flow matches the 20 cm/s setting. Pre-test flow visualization (F1C1/F1C2 and F2C1/F2C2 in the table) is typically used to check the flow field before ignition. The record does not say what it was used for.

## PSI-98 | 7. Inconsistencies
In the PSI-98 record:
1. The record title ("Characterization of Smoke from Microgravity Fires for Improved Spacecraft Fire Detection") is about smoke, but the text describes the Saffire-1 large-scale fire experiment.
2. The dates (03-02-2001 to 02-28-2008) are the same as in PSI-102 (SAME, smoke). The record does not give the date of the Saffire-I run.
3. The Project table says ISS, while the Approach says the experiment ran on Cygnus after it deberthed from the ISS.
In the table:
4. Length is 40.6 cm and width is 94 cm. The record says about 0.94 m long and 0.4 m wide, so length and width are reversed. Check which dimension lies along the flow.
5. Thickness 0.37 cm (3.7 mm). The record gives no thickness. If the source meant 0.37 mm, the unit is wrong. Check the source.
6. Percent O2 is a range for S1 ("21.5 - 21.7") and approximate for S2 ("~ 21.5").
7. "Burn Time" is not defined (complete burn, flame duration, or time to extinction).
8. The record does not mention the opposed-flow test S2.

## PSI-98 | 9. Questions a team may ask
Q: What is PSI-98?
A: Saffire-I, a large-scale fire experiment on the Cygnus vehicle after it deberthed from the ISS. The record title refers to smoke characterization, but the text describes the fire experiment. [PSI]

Q: What were the objectives?
A: (1) Determine how rapidly a large-scale fire grows in low gravity. (2) Investigate the low-g flammability limits compared with those from NASA's normal-gravity material flammability screening test. [PSI]

Q: What is the hypothesis?
A: Because fire behavior differs greatly between normal and reduced gravity, using terrestrial fires and standards to design spacecraft fire safety systems carries inherent risk, and microgravity data are lacking. Large-scale tests in an expendable spacecraft address this gap without risk to a crew. [PSI]

Q: What material and size?
A: SIBAL, cotton on fiberglass (75/25 by weight), about 0.94 m x 0.4 m. [PSI]

Q: What was the flow speed?
A: 20 cm/s, drawn by fans through a flow duct. [PSI + TABLE]

Q: Which tests are in the table?
A: S1 (concurrent flow, burn time 420 s) and S2 (opposed flow, burn time 70 s), both at 20 cm/s, about 21.5% O2, 182 W for 8 s ignition. [TABLE]

Q: What was measured?
A: Flame images (two cameras), flow/flame/plume temperatures (thermocouples), flame radiation (two radiometers), pressure and CO2 and O2 mole fraction (solid state sensors), and flow velocity (two anemometers). [PSI]

Q: What were the results (spread rate, flammability limit)?
A: [N/A] The record and the table give none.

Q: Why did S1 burn longer than S2?
A: [N/A] The table does not say. Concurrent and opposed flow are different flame-spread regimes, but the table does not give spread rates or say whether the sample burned completely. [PHYS]

Q: Is the table consistent with the record?
A: The flow speed matches. Length and width are reversed (40.6 cm x 94 cm vs about 0.94 m x 0.4 m), the thickness unit should be checked, and the record does not mention the opposed-flow test. [TABLE + PSI]

Q: How does Saffire-I differ from Saffire-II and Saffire-III?
A: See table 8.1: Saffire-II has 9 samples of 5 cm x 29 cm in several materials at 20 or 30 cm/s, and Saffire-III uses SIBAL at 30 cm/s. [PSI]

Q: How does Saffire relate to BASS?
A: Both address how material flammability in space differs from normal-gravity screening. Saffire uses a large sample in an unmanned vehicle, BASS uses small samples in the glovebox (table 8.2). [PSI]

Q: Is PSI-98 about smoke?
A: The title says so and the dates match PSI-102 (SAME), but the text describes the fire experiment. Smoke particle size is the subject of PSI-102 and PSI-101. [PSI]

Q: What is the benefit for Earth?
A: Fire safety and prevention in small sealed environments such as mines, airplanes, or submarines. [PSI]

## PSI-98 | 11. Impacts [PSI]
- A new way to study a realistic fire on an exploration vehicle, not possible before because the risks are too high.
- Instruments on an unmanned cargo vessel measure oxygen, carbon dioxide, heat, pressure, and flame growth, and two video cameras show the flame.
- Results determine microgravity flammability limits for several spacecraft materials, help validate NASA's material selection criteria, and show how microgravity and limited oxygen affect flame size.
- Crucial for the safety of current and future space missions. Studying fire in small sealed environments such as Cygnus benefits fire safety on Earth, including mines, airplanes, and submarines.
